Field notes / Personal research

Some of my losses. One of my wins.

Not every interesting response is a vulnerability. Not every convincing hypothesis survives independent review. The real skill is knowing the difference.

Disclosure boundary: These are reflections on my own investigation process. They do not reproduce confidential report submissions, live targets, original traffic or undisclosed findings. Detailed real-program reports are withheld pending explicit publication permission.

Loss 01 / Evidence disappears faster than a hypothesis

I learned that a surprising result is only the beginning. When I cannot recreate a sequence later, the most useful work is not a confident severity claim. It is documenting the state, timestamps, request context, controls and exactly what I observed, while admitting what cannot be independently checked.

What changed: I now prioritize a minimal replayable proof over adding more speculative attack steps.

Loss 02 / A broken assumption is not automatically demonstrated impact

Many identity and authorization investigations start with a valid question about a trust boundary. Independent review can still reject the claim when a missing precondition prevents an external attacker from completing the chain.

What changed: I separate the server behavior I observed from the attacker capabilities I would still need to prove.

Loss 03 / Scope and evidence are independent constraints

A test can be technically interesting yet fall outside a program's testing boundaries. Strong engineering work includes understanding what was permitted, which assumptions were tested and what remains unverified.

What changed: I check authorization, scope and program rules before building on intermediate behaviors.

The win / Follow-through matters

One experience strengthened my appreciation for the full reporting lifecycle: clear reproduction, triage feedback, retesting and communicating what changed. A convincing security report survives scrutiny and identifies the limits of its own evidence.

Private program-specific details are not reproduced here. I prefer an accurate public account of my methodology to an invented, full-looking exploit transcript.

What I can show publicly

For exact HTTP request/response pairs, code and regression tests, my original localhost authorization laboratory is fully reproducible. Its API deliberately contains known broken authorization decisions and their fixes, with fictional user accounts.
